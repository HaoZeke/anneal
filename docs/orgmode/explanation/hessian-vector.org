* Hessian-vector products

This page explains why a potential handle computes Hessian-vector products
itself and keeps a cached centre, how it reports the scheme that answered, and
when a caller should ask for the dense Hessian instead.
The functions, their arguments and their errors are in the
[[file:../reference/c_abi.org][C ABI reference]].

A minimum-mode search (Lanczos, dimer) applies the Hessian =H= of the energy to
many directions =v= at one geometry.
With only a force callback each product is a difference of two force
evaluations, and one of them is the centre the search has not moved from.
A potential handle answers =H v= itself and keeps the forces of that centre.

** Cost and error

| Scheme               | Force calls per product        | Error                 |
|----------------------+--------------------------------+-----------------------|
| =RGPOT_HVP_FORWARD=  | 1, plus 1 for each new centre  | =O(h)=                |
| =RGPOT_HVP_CENTRAL=  | 2                              | =O(h^2)=              |
| =RGPOT_HVP_ANALYTIC= | 0                              | the callback's        |
| =RGPOT_HVP_AD=       | 0                              | rounding of the duals |

The forward scheme reuses the forces of the centre.
=k= forward products at one centre therefore cost =k + 1= force calls instead of
=2k=.
No finite difference makes one call per product at =O(h^2)=.
The forward error is =(h/2) T(u, u) |v|=, with =T= the third derivative of the
energy and =u = v / |v|=, and cancelling it takes the second evaluation that the
central scheme makes.
A search that needs second-order products at no extra force call registers an
analytic product.

=h= is the displacement along the unit direction =u=, in the units of the
positions, and the difference is scaled back by =|v|=.
The forward product is therefore positively homogeneous in =v= but not odd: the
=O(h)= term keeps its sign when =v= changes sign.

** The cached centre

The handle keys its centre on the bits of the positions, the atomic numbers and
the cell.
Comparing bits rather than values keeps the forces computed at =0.0= from being
served at =-0.0=.
Moving one coordinate by one ulp, changing a species or changing the cell is a
new centre.

The centre is the entry of the handle's fused cache, which the eindir energy and
gradient share, and it stores the outcome of the force call: the forces, or the
failure.
A centre that failed, because the callback returned an error status or an SCC
did not converge, is returned again for the same bits and settings without
another call.
A failed energy is not retried at the same bits: a warm-start retry needs a
changed setting, installed with =rgpot_potential_set_fused_settings=, or a call
to =rgpot_potential_hvp_invalidate=.

The key does not see the state behind the callback's =user_data=, apart from the
settings the potential reports.
A potential whose other parameters change at a fixed geometry calls
=rgpot_potential_hvp_invalidate= to drop the centre.

A search usually needs the centre forces as well as the products.
=rgpot_potential_hvp_centre= returns them from the same cache, so the force call
that the search makes at the centre is the one the forward products reuse.
The handle holds one centre, which matches a search that stays at one geometry
for many products and then moves.

** Analytic and dual-number products

A potential that knows its Hessian registers the product with
=rgpot_potential_set_hvp_callback=.
=RGPOT_HVP_AUTO= then uses it and makes no force call.
A potential whose energy kernel is written over a scalar template can instead
evaluate it on second-order dual numbers, which carry a value, a first and a
second directional derivative through every operation.
The second derivative along =v= is exact up to the rounding of that arithmetic,
with no step to choose;
=rgpot_potential_set_ad_hvp_callback= registers that product, and =RGPOT_HVP_AD=
asks for it.
=RGPOT_HVP_AUTO= never takes the dual-number product, so a potential that has
both keeps its closed form.

In C++, =rgpot::PotentialHandle::from_impl= registers each hook a potential
advertises in =caps()=: =hessianVectorImpl= for =analyticHvp=,
=hessianVectorAdImpl= for =adHvp=, =hessianImpl= for =analyticHessian= and
=hessianAdImpl= for =adHessian=.
=MorsePot=, =LJPot= and the Fortran =CuH2Pot= have an analytic product,
=MorsePot= and =LJPot= an analytic Hessian, and =LJPot= both dual-number hooks.

** The scheme that ran

=RGPOT_HVP_AUTO= takes the analytic product when there is one and forward
differences otherwise.
That fallback is kept, but it is never silent:
=rgpot_potential_hvp_resolved= writes the scheme that ran, =rgpot::PotentialHandle::hvp= and the Python =hvp_resolved=
return it, and =rgpot_potential_derivative_counts= counts every product under
it.
A caller for which a finite-difference stand-in would be a wrong answer, rather
than a slower one, asks for =RGPOT_HVP_EXACT=: the analytic product, else the
dual-number one, and =RGPOT_INVALID_PARAMETER= before any call when the handle
has neither.
=RGPOT_HVP_ANALYTIC= and =RGPOT_HVP_AD= are refused the same way when their
callback is missing.

** Dense Hessians

=rgpot_potential_hessian= writes the =3N= square Hessian, row-major.
It has no automatic scheme, so a dense Hessian is differenced only when the
caller names =RGPOT_HESSIAN_CENTRAL=.

| Scheme                   | Force calls  | Error                 |
|--------------------------+--------------+-----------------------|
| =RGPOT_HESSIAN_ANALYTIC= | 0            | the callback's        |
| =RGPOT_HESSIAN_AD=       | 0            | rounding of the duals |
| =RGPOT_HESSIAN_EXACT=    | 0            | that of the one taken |
| =RGPOT_HESSIAN_CENTRAL=  | =6N=         | =O(h^2)=              |

The central scheme displaces each coordinate by =+-h=, so column =c= is
=(F(x - h e_c) - F(x + h e_c)) / (2 h)=, and it writes the symmetric part of the
differenced matrix.
Its calls bypass the cached centre and are counted apart from those of the
products.
=RGPOT_HESSIAN_EXACT= reports the scheme it took, like =RGPOT_HVP_EXACT=.

** Capabilities

=rgpot_potential_derivative_caps= reports, in one plain struct, which schemes a
handle has for the product and for the Hessian: an analytic and a dual-number
flag for each, set by the registered callbacks, and a finite-difference flag
that every handle sets.
It also gives the force calls the differenced schemes cost: one per forward
product at the cached centre, two per central product and six per atom for a
central Hessian.
The analytic and dual-number schemes make no force call, and their cost is that
of the potential, which the struct does not guess.

** Products or a dense Hessian

A caller that needs =k= products at one geometry pays
=t_hvp= for the first, which includes the centre or any other preparation, and =t_apply=
for each further one, against =t_dense= for the Hessian, after which every
product is a matrix-vector multiply.
The products are cheaper while =k < k*=, with
\[
k^* = \frac{t_\mathrm{dense} - t_\mathrm{hvp}}{t_\mathrm{apply}} + 1 .
\]
With finite differences only, counted in force calls,
=t_dense = 6N=, =t_hvp = 2= and =t_apply = 1=, so =k* = 6N - 1=: a dimer
rotation or a Lanczos search of a few tens of products stays on the products for
any but the smallest system.
With analytic or dual-number hooks the three times are those of the potential;
for a pair potential the product and the Hessian both cost a pass over the
pairs, so =k*= is small and a search that needs more than a handful of products
at one geometry, or the full spectrum, should take the Hessian.
The rule counts each multiply by the stored Hessian as free.
That multiply reads all =(3N)^2= entries, which for a pair potential with every
pair in range costs a large fraction of a product, so a caller near =k*= should
time it too: with
=t_mv= per multiply, the products are cheaper while =k < (t_dense - t_hvp + t_apply) / (t_apply - t_mv)=,
and always when =t_mv >= t_apply=.
No scheme is chosen for the caller: the handle reports the schemes and their
force-call costs, and the caller applies the rule to its own timings.

** Without the Rust core

=CppCore/rgpot/HessianVector.hpp= gives the same schemes, default step, centre
cache and counters to C++ potentials used without the Rust core.
=rgpot::HessianVectorCache::apply= returns the scheme that ran,
=rgpot::HessianVectorCache::hessian= takes an explicit =rgpot::HessianScheme=,
and =rgpot::derivativeCaps= reads the =caps()= bits of a potential.
The Python module uses it.
